🛷 Newton's Second Law — F = ma

Newton's first law says things keep doing what they're doing unless pushed. The second law says exactly how much a push changes their motion: force equals mass times acceleration. A force doesn't set an object's speed — it sets its acceleration, and heavier objects (more mass, more inertia) accelerate less for the same push. Shove the block below and watch a = F/m.

The ideas

Force → acceleration, not speed

A steady force doesn't hold a steady speed — it steadily changes speed. Keep pushing on frictionless ice and the block keeps speeding up. Remove the force and it coasts (First Law).

Mass is inertia

The same push on a heavier block produces less acceleration — a = F/m. Double the mass, halve the acceleration. That resistance to being sped up is what mass measures.

Only the net force matters

Add up every force — your push, friction, gravity — as vectors. It's the leftover net force that accelerates the object. If pushes cancel, a = 0 and velocity holds steady.

F = dp/dt

Newton really wrote force as the rate of change of momentum (p = mv). That's why a rocket losing mass, or a longer collision time (crumple zones, airbags), changes the forces you feel.

This one equation runs the everyday mechanical world: why a loaded truck needs more road to stop, why a lighter bat swings faster, why rockets need enormous thrust to accelerate their huge mass. Pair it with the first law (inertia) and the third (equal and opposite reactions) and you can predict the motion of almost anything at human scale.
