🤝 Game Theory — the Prisoner's Dilemma

Game theory is the math of decisions that depend on other people's decisions. Its most famous puzzle is the Prisoner's Dilemma: two players each choose to cooperate or defect. Whatever the other does, defecting always scores you more points — so cold logic says defect. Yet if both defect, both do worse than if both had cooperated. Axelrod's tournament — do nice guys finish last?

In 1980 Robert Axelrod had game-theory experts submit strategies and pitted them against each other in a round-robin of repeated Prisoner's Dilemmas. Run it below: each strategy plays every other for 150 rounds. Watch Always Defect — it never loses a single duel, yet look where it lands.

The ideas behind it

Dominant strategy & Nash equilibrium

Defecting beats cooperating no matter what the other does, so it's dominant. Both reason that way and both defect — a Nash equilibrium nobody can improve alone, even though it's worse for both than mutual cooperation.

Non-zero-sum

Unlike chess, this isn't win-lose. Both can gain (3,3) or both lose (1,1). Cooperation creates value; the dilemma is that self-interest destroys it.

The shadow of the future

Play once and defection rules. But when the game repeats and you'll meet again, reciprocity pays: cooperate, but punish defection. That's why Tit-for-Tat thrives.

Axelrod's winner

The champions were nice (never defect first), retaliating, forgiving, and clear. Always Defect wins every head-to-head but finishes near the bottom — it can never build the cooperation that racks up points.

This one little game explains an astonishing amount of the world: arms races, overfishing, price wars, climate negotiations, vaccination, and even how cooperation evolved among selfish genes. Whenever short-term self-interest clashes with collective good, you're looking at a Prisoner's Dilemma.
