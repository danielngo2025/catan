📐 The Einstein Field Equations — geometry = matter

General relativity's whole content sits in one line. The left side describes the curvature of spacetime (geometry); the right side is the matter and energy in it. They're set equal — so, as John Wheeler put it, "matter tells spacetime how to curve; spacetime tells matter how to move." Applied to the entire universe, the equations become the Friedmann equation, and the balance of ordinary matter against the cosmological constant Λ (dark energy) decides the fate of everything. Inside the equation

Two sides, one balance

Left (blue): the curvature tensor built from the metric — the shape of spacetime. Right (amber): the stress-energy tensor — density, momentum, and pressure. It's really ten coupled equations, and the coupling 8πG/c⁴ is tiny, so it takes a planet's worth of mass to bend space noticeably.

The cosmological constant Λ

Einstein added Λ to force a static universe, then called it his "biggest blunder" once expansion was discovered. It returned in 1998 as dark energy — ~68% of the cosmos — the accelerator you feel when Ω_Λ dominates above.

Its solutions are reality

Solve it and out come black holes (Schwarzschild), the expanding universe (Friedmann–Lemaître, plotted here), and gravitational waves — ripples confirmed by LIGO in 2015, a century after the prediction.

The cosmic tug-of-war

Gravity from matter pulls the expansion to a halt (and maybe a Big Crunch); dark energy pushes it to accelerate. If they balance and Ω total = 1, space is flat. Our universe sits almost exactly there.

The equations are famously hard to solve — nonlinear, because spacetime's own curvature carries energy that curves spacetime further. Yet every precision test, from Mercury's orbit to GPS to black-hole mergers, has confirmed them. One elegant line, written in 1915, still sets the destiny of the entire universe.
