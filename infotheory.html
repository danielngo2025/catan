📡 Information Theory — measuring information in bits

In 1948 Claude Shannon asked a strange question: how do you measure information? His answer founded the digital age. Information is the reduction of uncertainty, and its natural unit is the bit — one yes/no answer. A message is surprising (carries lots of information) when it's unlikely, and boring (carries little) when it's predictable. The average surprise of a source is its entropy. The big ideas

Entropy = average surprise

A symbol with probability p carries −log₂(p) bits of "surprise." Rare events surprise you more. Entropy averages that surprise — it's the minimum bits/symbol you can't beat.

A bit is one question

Each yes/no question can at best halve the possibilities, so identifying one of N equally likely things takes log₂N bits. 20 questions ⇒ ~1 million options.

Compression = removing redundancy

Shannon's source-coding theorem: you can pack a source down to its entropy, no further. Give frequent symbols short codes (like Morse's "E") and you win. That's ZIP, MP3, JPEG.

Noise, capacity & error-correction

Every channel has a maximum reliable rate — its capacity. Astonishingly, Shannon proved you can transmit error-free up to that limit by adding cleverly redundant codes. That's why deep-space photos and QR codes survive noise.

The redundancy is everywhere: written English runs at only about 1 bit per letter despite 26 letters (log₂26 ≈ 4.7), because "q" is almost always followed by "u" and you can often guess the next letter. That slack is exactly what compression exploits — and what makes languages robust to typos. One equation, H = −Σ p log₂ p, underlies all of it: phones, the internet, DNA, and every file you've ever saved.
