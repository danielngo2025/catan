⛰️ Gradient Descent — how machines learn

Training a neural network means finding the settings that make its error (loss) as small as possible. With billions of knobs you can't just try combinations — so instead you feel the slope of the loss and step downhill, over and over. That's gradient descent. The single most important setting is the learning rate — the step size. Too small and training crawls; too big and it overshoots and blows up; and a bumpy landscape can trap you in a local minimum. The core of all training

Follow the slope

The gradient points in the direction the loss rises fastest, so stepping the opposite way lowers it. Repeat and you slide toward a valley — a minimum of the loss. No search, just downhill.

The learning-rate knob

Too small (crawls, needs forever) or too big (leaps over the valley and bounces or diverges). Getting it right is the central art of training; real systems even schedule it to shrink over time.

Local minima

A wiggly landscape has valleys that aren't the deepest. Start on the right and you settle into a shallow local minimum; start on the left and you find the deeper global one. Where you begin matters.

Billions of dimensions

Here w is one number; a real network has billions, and "downhill" is computed for all of them at once by backpropagation. Astonishingly, in very high dimensions bad local minima become rare — part of why deep learning works.

This one loop — compute the gradient, take a small step downhill, repeat — trains virtually every modern AI, from image classifiers to the LLMs behind ChatGPT and Claude. The magic isn't the algorithm (it's 200 years old); it's doing it across billions of parameters and oceans of data.
