🌀 Quantum Mechanics — the double-slit experiment

Fire tiny particles — electrons, photons — one at a time at a wall with two slits. Each lands as a single dot, like a bullet. Yet let thousands pile up and they form interference fringes — bright and dark bands, as if each particle were a wave passing through both slits at once. Now switch on a which-path detector to catch which slit each one uses… and the fringes vanish. Watching the particle changes what it does. This is the heart of quantum weirdness.

What you're seeing

Superposition

With the detector off, each particle doesn't pick a slit — it travels as a wave of possibilities through both, and the two paths interfere with themselves.

Wave–particle duality

It arrives as one localized dot (particle), but where it's likely to land is set by a wave. Neither picture alone is complete.

The Born rule

The wave gives a probability, not a path. Bright fringes = high odds, dark = near-zero. Any single dot is random; only the crowd reveals the pattern.

Measurement collapse

Detecting the path forces the particle to be at one slit. The superposition is destroyed, the interference disappears, and you get two plain clumps.

The unsettling part isn't that particles are wavy — it's that observation itself changes the outcome. You can't sneak a look at which slit is used without erasing the very interference that proves it went through both. Richard Feynman called this the one mystery that holds "the heart of quantum mechanics."
