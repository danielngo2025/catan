⚛️ Heisenberg's Uncertainty Principle

Δx · Δp ≥ ℏ / 2

A quantum particle is a wave, not a tiny dot — so it can't have a single exact position and a single exact momentum at the same time. Squeeze it into a small region (know its position well) and its momentum smears out across many values, and vice-versa. This isn't clumsy measuring equipment; it's built into what a particle is. The part everyone gets wrong

The common story — "measuring the position bumps the particle and changes its momentum" — is real (the observer effect) but it's not what this principle says. Even a perfect, gentle measurement hits the same wall, because the particle simply doesn't possess a definite position and momentum simultaneously. A wave that's sharply localized in space is necessarily built from many different wavelengths — and wavelength is momentum. Narrow in space ⇒ broad in momentum. It's the same math that says a very short sound click contains a wide range of frequencies.

ℏ (h-bar) is tiny (~10⁻³⁴ J·s), which is why you never notice this for a baseball — but for an electron it dominates everything. The chart uses natural units where ℏ = 1, so the floor Δx·Δp = ½ is the best nature ever allows; real particles sit at or above it.
