🐱 Schrödinger's Equation — the wavefunction

This is the master equation of quantum mechanics. A particle isn't a dot with a position — it's a spread-out wavefunction ψ, and Schrödinger's equation says how ψ flows and changes in time. You never see ψ directly; its square |ψ|² gives the probability of finding the particle somewhere (the Born rule). Two of its strangest predictions: energy that comes in discrete levels, and particles that tunnel through walls they can't classically cross. Explore both below.

What the equation reveals

Energy is quantized

Confine a wave and only whole numbers of half-wavelengths fit — so only special energies E ∝ n² are allowed. This "particle in a box" is why atoms have discrete energy levels and emit sharp spectral colours.

|ψ|² is probability

The wavefunction itself is complex and unobservable; Max Born's rule says |ψ|² is the probability density. A stationary state has a fixed |ψ|² even though ψ keeps rotating in phase.

Quantum tunnelling

A wave packet meeting a barrier taller than its energy still leaks partly through — impossible for a classical ball. It powers nuclear fusion in the Sun, alpha decay, flash memory, and the scanning tunnelling microscope.

Superposition & time

Add two energy states and |ψ|² sloshes back and forth — the "beat" between their energies. The equation is linear, so any sum of solutions is also a solution: the heart of quantum weirdness.

Schrödinger's equation is to quantum mechanics what Newton's laws are to everyday motion — give it the forces (the potential V) and it predicts the future of ψ exactly and deterministically. The randomness enters only when you measure: then |ψ|² tells you the odds. Dirac later made it compatible with relativity; this non-relativistic form still governs chemistry, materials, and every quantum device.
