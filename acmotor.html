🔄 The Rotating Magnetic Field — Tesla's AC Motor

In 1882 Nikola Tesla had a flash of insight that runs half the machines on Earth. Feed three AC currents that peak one after another (each a third of a cycle apart) into three sets of coils, and their combined magnetic field doesn't just wobble — it rotates smoothly on its own, with no moving parts. Drop a metal rotor inside and the spinning field drags it along. That's the AC induction motor: rugged, brushless, and everywhere from fans to factories to EVs. Watch the field spin below.

Why it was revolutionary

Three phases, one spin

The three currents peak in turn, so the "strongest" coil hands off to the next around the ring. Their vector sum is a constant-strength field that sweeps around at the line frequency — motion conjured from stationary coils.

No brushes, no contacts

Earlier DC motors needed sliding brushes and a commutator that sparked and wore out. Tesla's motor induces current in the rotor wirelessly via the rotating field — almost nothing to break.

Slip drives the rotor

The rotor always spins a touch slower than the field (that lag is "slip"). It has to: only a changing field induces the rotor currents that produce torque. No slip, no pull.

Flip two wires, flip the spin

Swap any two of the three phases and the field reverses direction instantly — how every three-phase machine goes into reverse. Try the button above.

This one idea — paired with AC's easy voltage transformation — won the "War of the Currents" and electrified the world. The same rotating field runs in reverse inside every generator at every power plant. Tesla reportedly saw the whole design in his mind, fully formed, while reciting poetry at sunset.
