🔗 Attention — how a transformer connects words

Before transformers, models read text strictly one word after another and quickly forgot the start of a long sentence. Attention changed everything: to understand each word, the model looks back at every other word at once and decides how much each one matters — how much to "attend" to it. That's how it resolves what a pronoun refers to, or links a verb to its subject. Click any word below to see where its attention goes. (Start with "it".)

Why attention was the breakthrough

Look at everything at once

Each word forms a "query" and compares it against a "key" from every other word; the match scores become attention weights. Nothing is out of reach, so meaning can flow directly between distant words.

Resolving reference

Click "it": its attention lands hard on "cat". The model has learned that "it" here means the cat — the kind of context-tracking that makes generated text coherent.

Parallel, not sequential

Because every word attends to every other simultaneously, the whole sentence is processed in parallel. That's what let transformers scale to enormous size on modern hardware — the "T" in GPT.

Many heads

Real models run dozens of attention "heads" at once, each learning a different relationship — grammar, reference, topic. Stacked over many layers, they build the rich understanding an LLM draws on.

The 2017 paper that introduced this was literally titled "Attention Is All You Need," and it underpins essentially every modern LLM. The weights shown here are illustrative (hand-set to mirror what a trained model does); a real transformer computes them from learned query/key vectors for a vocabulary of tens of thousands of tokens.
