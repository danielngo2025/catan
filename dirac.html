🪞 The Dirac Equation — antimatter from pure logic

Schrödinger's equation isn't compatible with Einstein's relativity. In 1928 Paul Dirac found the one that is — and it paid off spectacularly. To work, it needed four components, and out of them spin-½ dropped out automatically (no longer bolted on by hand). Stranger still, it had negative-energy solutions. Dirac's bold reading: empty space is a filled "sea" of them, and a hole in that sea is a new particle — antimatter. He predicted the positron in 1928; it was found in 1932. Why it changed everything

Quantum ⋈ relativity

Schrödinger's equation treats time and space differently, breaking relativity. Dirac's is symmetric in both — the first quantum equation that respects the speed-of-light limit for the electron.

Spin comes free

The maths demands four components. Two are the electron's spin-up/down; the equation even predicts the magnetic strength g ≈ 2, which earlier theory had simply inserted by hand. Spin is a relativistic effect.

The negative-energy sea

Relativity's E = ±√((pc)² + (mc²)²) allows negative energies. Dirac proposed the vacuum is a filled sea of them; a missing electron leaves a hole that looks like a positive-charge particle — the positron.

Every particle has a twin

Antimatter turned out to be universal: antiprotons, antineutrons, and more, all with opposite charge. Matter meeting antimatter annihilates into pure energy — PET scanners image your body with exactly this.

Dirac trusted his equation over common sense, and reality obeyed — a landmark case of pure mathematics predicting a whole new form of matter before anyone looked. His work also seeded quantum field theory: the modern view keeps the antimatter and the pair-creation but drops the literal "sea," treating electrons and positrons as excitations of one quantum field. Why the universe ended up mostly matter and almost no antimatter is still an open mystery.
