⚡ The Planck–Einstein Relation — E = hf

Planck's quanta were a maths trick until Einstein took them literally in 1905: light itself comes in packets — photons — each carrying energy E = hf, proportional to its frequency, not its brightness. The proof is the photoelectric effect: shine light on a metal and electrons fly off, but only if the light's frequency is high enough. A dim blue beam works; a blindingly bright red one does nothing. Classical waves couldn't explain that — photons can. Try it below.

Why this broke classical physics

Frequency, not brightness

A classical wave should dump more energy into electrons as you brighten it. Instead, brightness only changes how many electrons come out — their energy depends solely on the light's frequency. Only "one photon, one electron" explains that.

The threshold

Below a cut-off frequency f₀ = φ/h, nothing is ejected however bright the light — because no single photon has enough energy (hf < φ) to free an electron. Cross the threshold and it switches on instantly.

Energy budget

Each freed electron leaves with kinetic energy KE = hf − φ: the photon's energy minus the "work function" φ needed to escape the metal. More frequency ⇒ faster electrons; more brightness ⇒ more electrons.

Wave–particle duality

Light diffracts and interferes like a wave, yet here it plainly acts like a stream of particles. Both are true. Einstein's photon idea — not relativity — is what won him the Nobel Prize.

E = hf is the bridge between a wave property (frequency) and a particle property (energy). It sets the energy of every photon from radio to gamma, underlies solar cells, digital cameras, and photosynthesis, and — paired with de Broglie's converse, that particles also have a wavelength — completes the wave–particle picture at the heart of quantum mechanics.
