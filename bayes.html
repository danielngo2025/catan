🎯 Bayes' Theorem — the false-positive surprise

Bayes' theorem is the rule for updating a belief when new evidence arrives: your prior probability, reweighted by how well the evidence fits. Its most famous lesson is deeply counter-intuitive. Suppose a disease affects 1% of people and there's a test that's ~99% accurate. You test positive — how likely are you to actually be sick? Most people guess ~99%. The real answer is often under 20%. How the update works

Prior → posterior

Bayes multiplies your prior belief by the "likelihood" of the evidence and renormalizes: P(sick | +) = P(+ | sick)·P(sick) ⁄ P(+). Evidence nudges the prior; it doesn't replace it.

Base rates dominate

With a rare disease there are ~99× more healthy people, so even a small false-positive rate produces a flood of false alarms that swamps the few true positives. Ignoring the base rate is the "base-rate fallacy."

Evidence stacks up

Today's posterior is tomorrow's prior. A second independent positive test updates again and pushes the probability much higher — which is exactly why doctors confirm with a second, different test.

Bayes is everywhere

Spam filters, medical screening, search-and-rescue, machine learning, and scientific inference all run on it. Misused in court it becomes the "prosecutor's fallacy" — confusing P(evidence | innocent) with P(innocent | evidence).

The grid makes the counter-intuitive feel obvious: a "99% accurate" test sounds airtight, but accuracy on the test is not the same as the probability of being sick given a positive. Those two only converge when the condition is common. Bayes' theorem is, in the end, just careful bookkeeping about which population a piece of evidence came from.
