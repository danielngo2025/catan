🔌 AC vs DC — the War of the Currents

In the 1880s Edison pushed DC and Tesla & Westinghouse pushed AC for powering cities. The physics that decided it is one formula: power lost heating the wires is I²R. Send the same power at a higher voltage and the current I drops, so the loss plummets with the square. AC's killer advantage: a transformer can cheaply crank voltage up to hundreds of thousands of volts for the long haul, then back down for your home. DC couldn't. Why AC won

Loss = I²R

Every wire has resistance R, and pushing current I through it wastes I²R as heat. To move a fixed amount of power, you can use high voltage with low current, or low voltage with high current — and low current wins, hugely, because the loss goes as the current squared.

Transformers need AC

A transformer changes voltage using a changing magnetic field — which only alternating current provides. Steady DC can't be stepped up or down simply, so Edison was stuck transmitting near household voltage.

Edison's one-mile problem

At ~110–240 V the current is so large that DC lines lose everything within about a mile, forcing a power station in every neighborhood. AC substations feed whole regions from one distant plant.

Up for the road, down for the home

The grid steps up to hundreds of kV between cities, then down to 120/240 V at your street. High voltage is efficient but dangerous, so it never enters your house.

DC did eventually get its revenge: modern HVDC lines (using power electronics unavailable in Edison's day) now carry bulk power across continents with even lower loss than AC. But for building the everyday grid — generators, transformers, motors — Tesla's alternating current was the decisive winner.
