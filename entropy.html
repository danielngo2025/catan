⏳ The Law of Entropy — why disorder always wins

The Second Law says entropy — disorder — only ever increases. But why? Boltzmann's answer is pure counting: S = k · log W, where W is the number of microscopic arrangements that look the same from outside. There are staggeringly more mixed-up arrangements than tidy ones, so a system wandering at random almost always ends up mixed. Below, balls hop between two urns by a rule that's perfectly reversible — yet they march to 50/50 and never come back. Start it and watch the arrow of time appear.

The idea in four parts

S = k log W

A "macrostate" (say 40 balls in A) can be made from a huge number of "microstates" (which exact balls). Entropy just counts them — on a log scale. It's literally carved on Boltzmann's tombstone.

Disorder = higher odds

"All in A" is one arrangement; "half and half" has more arrangements than atoms in the galaxy. Pick at random and you'll land in the huge pile every time. Entropy rises because mixed states are overwhelmingly more probable.

The reversibility paradox

Each move — one random ball to the other urn — is perfectly reversible; run it backward and it's still valid. So why doesn't it un-mix? Because returning to "all in A" is possible but so unlikely you'd wait longer than the age of the universe.

The arrow of time

This statistical one-way drift is why the past differs from the future. It's why heat flows hot→cold, why eggs don't unscramble, and why the far-future universe drifts toward featureless "heat death."

Notice the deep move: the microscopic laws are reversible and have no arrow of time, yet the macroscopic world clearly does. Entropy resolves this — the arrow isn't in the laws, it's in the counting, plus the fact that the universe started in an extraordinarily low-entropy (ordered) state. Everything since has been the sand running down.
