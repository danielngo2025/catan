🔥 Planck's Law — the birth of the quantum

Every warm object glows: embers red, the Sun white, the hottest stars blue. In 1900 Max Planck found the exact equation for that glow — the black-body spectrum. To make it fit, he had to assume energy comes only in discrete quanta, E = hf, an act he called desperate. It worked perfectly, slayed a paradox that had broken classical physics, and accidentally launched quantum mechanics. Slide the temperature and watch the curve — and the colour — move.

Why it mattered

The ultraviolet catastrophe

Classical physics (the dashed red curve) predicted a hot body pours out infinite energy at short wavelengths — obviously wrong. It was one of the great cracks in 19th-century physics.

Planck's fix: energy in lumps

Planck assumed light of frequency f can only carry energy in packets of E = hf. High-frequency (short-λ) packets are too expensive to emit, so the curve turns over instead of exploding. Quantization was born.

Wien's law: colour = temperature

The peak wavelength is inversely proportional to T. That's why you can read a star's temperature from its colour, and why heating metal glows red → orange → white → blue.

Stefan–Boltzmann: T⁴

Total power radiated scales as temperature to the fourth power — double the temperature and you get 16× the energy. A small rise in T makes an object dramatically brighter.

Planck thought his energy quanta were just a mathematical trick. Five years later Einstein took them literally to explain the photoelectric effect (that's a real photon), and the quantum revolution was unstoppable. The same curve today gives us the temperature of the Sun, of stars, of a furnace — and of the cosmic microwave background, the Big Bang's leftover glow at a chilly 2.7 K.
